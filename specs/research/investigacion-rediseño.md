# Research Brief: Academic Evidence for Acéntrate Finanzas' Agency-Based Spending Framework (2015–2025)

## TL;DR
- **The core philosophy is partially validated but needs two corrections.** Recent research strongly supports the idea that *perceived control / locus of control* is a genuine driver of saving and spending behavior — making the agency-based framing better-grounded than the conventional housing/food/transport taxonomy. But the same literature shows that (a) spending categorization *by itself* produces weak, null, or even *negative* behavior change, and (b) agency-style labels can backfire by either licensing overspending ("I deserve this — it was discretionary, my choice") or trapping money in "sacred" earmarked accounts while users take on costly debt.
- **The strongest evidence-based wins** for the app are: organizing expenses around *controllability* (internal locus of control reliably predicts higher savings); treating **social/prosocial and time-saving spending as legitimately distinct "good" discretionary categories** (these have well-replicated wellbeing benefits that ordinary discretionary spending lacks); and using AI categorization to *reduce friction* — provided the app preserves a moment of user reflection, since reflection (not the label) is what changes behavior.
- **The biggest research-flagged risks** are: the "budgeting app trap" (apps that surface "money left to spend" can *increase* spending); the "investment-in-self" self-licensing loophole (motivated reasoning to relabel hedonic spending as virtuous investment); and over-automation removing the reflective engagement that drives change. For **Colombia specifically**, high present-orientation, income volatility, informal saving, and conspicuous-consumption pressure mean the philosophy must be *localized*, not transplanted from US/European studies.

## Key Findings

**1. Categorization alone is a weak lever; reflection and friction matter more than the taxonomy.** In a fintech RCT run by Irrational Labs with Duke's Common Cents Lab, 9,035 users of the personal-finance app Clarity Money were randomized into an informational control, "one-number" budgeting, and category budgeting. Budgeting increased app engagement but, in the team's words, "overall we did not find positive or negative financial impacts from budgeting." This is the single most important caution: the *act of categorizing* is not self-evidently behavior-changing.

**2. Perceived control is the real mechanism — and this is the philosophy's strongest scientific foundation.** Cobb-Clark, Kassenboehmer & Sinning (2016, *Journal of Banking & Finance* 73:113–130) found that "households with an internal reference person save more both in terms of levels and as a percentage of their permanent incomes," with the wealth gap "particularly large for poor households." Financial self-efficacy research (Asebedo, 2019, and reviews building on it) concludes that FSE "focuses on perceived control over financial situations, which has a greater impact on behavior than actual control." Organizing expenses by *degree of control* therefore targets the variable that actually moves savings — a real advantage over type-based taxonomies.

**3. Spending categorization and tracking can backfire.** Two distinct backfire mechanisms appear in the literature. First, the "budgeting app trap" (a program of three field studies and two lab experiments summarized by the Behavioral Economics Institute) shows that "having access to budget information…can increase spending, particularly at the end of the budget period," because users treat the budgeted amount as money they are permitted to spend. Second, and most directly relevant to an agency/earmarking design: **Sussman & O'Brien (2016), "Knowing When to Spend: Unintended Financial Consequences of Earmarking to Encourage Savings," *Journal of Marketing Research* 53(5):790–803.** Across six experiments, they found that when savings are earmarked for a self-relevant purpose (e.g., a child's education), people "preserve that money even at a cost that exceeds the amount of that money itself" — i.e., they incur high-interest credit-card debt rather than spend the labeled savings. This is a direct warning for any app that makes categories feel sacred.

**4. Social, prosocial, and time-saving spending are genuinely distinct and deserve separate tracking.** Prosocial spending's link to wellbeing is robust though not unconditional. The high-powered registered replication by Aknin, Dunn, Proulx, Lok & Norton (2020, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 119(2):e15–e26) confirmed the effect that earlier small-sample studies (n<100/cell) had tested. Aknin, Dunn & Whillans (2022, *Current Directions in Psychological Science*) reaffirm it is "robust and replicable in large samples." Separately, **Whillans, Dunn, Smeets, Bekkers & Norton (2017, *PNAS* 114(32):8523–8527)** showed across samples from the US, Canada, Denmark and the Netherlands (n=6,271) that "individuals who spend money on time-saving services report greater life satisfaction," with a field experiment establishing causality. Because social/relational and time-saving spending carry wellbeing payoffs that generic discretionary spending does not, a separate "social" category is defensible — and arguably the app should also recognize time-saving purchases.

**5. The "investment in self" category is the most psychologically dangerous element.** Self-licensing research (de Witt Huberts, Evers & de Ridder, 2014, *Frontiers in Psychology*) demonstrates that "higher levels of temptation predicted the number of reasons employed and construed to justify consumption" — people actively manufacture justifications for indulgence. Motivated-reasoning work (Mishra and colleagues; and the saving/spending studies in the *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology / Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* tradition) shows vague or flexible categories give "greater latitude for generating justifications." Cheema & Soman (2006), "Malleable Mental Accounting" (*Journal of Consumer Psychology* 16(1):33–44), show people exploit categorization flexibility to justify attractive spending. A category literally labeled "investment" is an open invitation to relabel a restaurant meal, gadget, or trip as self-investment.

**6. AI categorization aids engagement but introduces automation and friction risks.** Algorithm aversion is *weaker* than commonly feared in financial domains — experiments find consumers weigh cost, accuracy, and stakes fairly rationally and do not reflexively reject algorithms (Brookings; *Journal of Business Economics*, 2022). However, over-reliance/automation complacency is a documented risk in robo-advisory, and — critically — the reflection that drives behavior change is exactly what automation removes. A study on financial self-control strategies (de Ridder group, *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, 2021) found that **generating one's own strategies reduced spending more than learning expert-provided ones**, underscoring that user agency in the process itself has value.

**7. Colombia requires genuine adaptation, not transplantation.** Colombia has Latin America's highest fintech adoption (76% per EY's 2019 index), but adoption is shallow and saving is weak and informal (details below). High present-orientation and income volatility mean the "income" and "committed" categories must accommodate irregular, informal earnings and biweekly ("quincena") cash cycles.

## Details

### Angle 1 — Does spending categorization change financial behavior?
The post-2015 evidence is mixed-to-skeptical, which should temper any claim that categorization is inherently transformative.

- **Null/negative results:** The Irrational Labs–Duke Common Cents Lab RCT (N=9,035, fintech partner Clarity Money) found the budgeting feature increased engagement but produced no measurable change in spending. A university RCT (N=177; *Journal of Financial Counseling and Planning*-adjacent, ERIC EJ1280271) found savings tools improved financial *satisfaction* and subjective perceptions but did "not significantly affect financial behaviors or objective financial wellness." The "budgeting app trap" research found budget-standing information can *increase* end-of-period spending.
- **Positive results:** **French, McKillop & Stewart (2020), "The effectiveness of smartphone apps in improving financial capability," *European Journal of Finance* 26(4–5):302–318** — an RCT with members of Derry Credit Union (Northern Ireland) using four bundled "Money Matters" apps — found improved financial knowledge, self-confidence, ability to delay gratification, and an increased "sense of being able to effect change." Their follow-up (*Strategic Change*, 2021) adds that engagement improves with targeting, push-notification personalization, and gamification.
- **Latin America RCT:** Frisancho, Herrera & Prina (2023), "Can a mobile-app-based behavioral intervention teach financial skills to youth?", *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* vol. 214:595–614 — a financial-diaries study with Peruvian youth — found the bundled app intervention improved financial literacy and price awareness but did "not lead to significant changes in shopping strategies, budgeting habits, or savings behavior," and actually *increased* credit usage. A direct caution that app-based tools can change knowledge without changing behavior — or can increase borrowing.
- **Theory update on mental accounting:** **Fei, Bartels & Zhang (2024), "Consumers' Mental Representation of Expenditures," Becker Friedman Institute Working Paper 2024-159** (seven studies plus analysis of ~6.5 million NielsenIQ grocery trips, 2008–2019) shows people represent expenditures in a *hierarchical taxonomy* and adjust spending based on "taxonomic distance," extending Thaler's binary topical/comprehensive-account model into a continuous, nested structure. Two findings matter for Acéntrate: (1) the consensus categories people spontaneously form are bills, groceries, household products, clothing, and entertainment — *type-based*, not agency-based — but (2) the two underlying dimensions the authors recover are roughly **necessity** and **typical spending amount**. In other words, *necessity/controllability is already a natural axis along which people mentally organize money* — which lends cognitive plausibility to the agency framing, even though people's default surface categories are type-based. The paper also explicitly advises that "personal finance apps can promote better consumption and budgeting behaviors when they better understand people's representation of expenditures" and that prompting "smaller, lower-level categories may help consumers restrict spending." **Supports the philosophy directionally; suggests the app should map agency labels onto users' natural necessity dimension rather than fight their type-based instincts.**

### Angle 2 — Agency-based vs. type-based categorization (strongest support)
- **Locus of control → savings:** Cobb-Clark, Kassenboehmer & Sinning (2016, *Journal of Banking & Finance* 73:113–130) and Bucciol & Trucchi (2020, Cardiff Economics Working Papers) find internal locus of control predicts more saving, partly via saving motives. Chu, Ngoc & Faizi (2022, *Annals of Economics and Finance* 23(2)), using Australian HILDA panel data, find "individuals with an internal locus of control are likely to save more, invest more, be more willing to take financial risks, and have less overdue payments."
- **Perceived control > actual control:** The financial self-efficacy literature (Asebedo, 2019; Farrell, Fry & Risse, 2016) repeatedly finds perceived control is more predictive of behavior than objective circumstances.
- **Controllable vs. uncontrollable framing exists in the literature:** The income-volatility study (*Frontiers in Psychology*, 2021; PMC8200394) explicitly distinguishes income shifts that are "controllable" (e.g., self-employed choosing contracts) vs. "uncontrollable" (agency-assigned shifts) and links perceived controllability to financial-planning behavior. **This is the clearest academic analogue to Acéntrate's "chose vs. had no option over" distinction and supports it directly.**

### Angle 3 — AI-assisted financial categorization
- **Algorithm aversion is weaker than feared in finance:** Germann & Merkle and related experiments (*Journal of Business Economics*, 2022) found "no sign of algorithm aversion" in delegated investing; Brookings consumer experiments found people choose algorithms when cheaper/more accurate and reserve human preference for high-stakes, subjective, or identity-relevant decisions. The MIS Quarterly integrative review (2024) and *Cyberpsychology* (2025) reframe the question around the trade-off between *decisional agency* and outcome quality — directly relevant to an app premised on agency.
- **Automation complacency / over-reliance** is documented in robo-advisory (multiple 2023–2026 reviews, incl. arXiv 2509.09922), centering on opacity, loss of perceived control, and over-trust in historical-data-based models.
- **Friction has value:** The financial self-control strategy study (de Ridder group, *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, 2021) found generating personal strategies reduced spending more than reading expert strategies, via greater perceived situation-fit. **Implication: auto-categorize to remove drudgery, but keep the user in the loop (confirm/override) so the reflective, agency-affirming moment survives.**

### Angle 4 — Social spending as a distinct category
- **Prosocial spending:** Aknin et al. (2020, *JPSP* 119(2):e15–e26) registered replication and Aknin, Dunn & Whillans (2022, *Current Directions in Psychological Science*) confirm robustness in large samples. Caveats to flag: Kim, Adams, Diaw, Celly, Nelson & Jung (2022, *PLOS One*, 10.1371/journal.pone.0272434) reran the original experiment and "did not observe a significant difference" on the original analysis (N=133), with effects appearing only on a more direct happiness measure; Falk & Graeber (2020, *PNAS* 117(12):6463–6468) documented "delayed negative effects of prosocial spending on happiness"; and Lok & Dunn (2022, *PLOS One*) examined moderators. The Aknin et al. (2023, *PMID 37377115*) systematic review synthesizes mediators/moderators.
- **Time-saving spending:** Whillans et al. (2017, *PNAS* 114(32):8523–8527) — n=6,271 across four countries plus a causal field experiment — established that buying time promotes happiness, with hints that benefits vary between higher- and lower-income countries (relevant to Colombia).
- **Takeaway:** A distinct "social/relational" category is well-justified because relational spending carries wellbeing returns ordinary discretionary spending lacks. **Recommend the app explicitly recognizes that "social" and "time-saving" spending are not failures of discipline but often high-return spending** — and consider whether time-saving belongs as its own tag.

### Angle 5 — Investment self-categorization bias
- Self-licensing: de Witt Huberts, Evers & de Ridder (2014, *Frontiers in Psychology*) — temptation drives the *construction* of justifications.
- Motivated reasoning in saving/spending: Mishra and colleagues show vague/flexible information "makes it easier for people to generate justifications to support their desired spending decisions," whereas a single, precise account "reduces the latitude for distortion."
- Malleable mental accounting: Cheema & Soman (2006, *Journal of Consumer Psychology* 16(1):33–44) — categorization flexibility is exploited to justify attractive spending.
- **Takeaway:** The "investment in self" category is the single most abusable label in the system. People will relabel hedonic consumption (dining, gadgets, travel, "wellness") as self-investment. **Recommend tight definitional guardrails, examples of what does/doesn't count, and possibly a confirmation prompt or a periodic "is this really investment?" review.**

### Angle 6 — Latin America / Colombia context
- **Adoption is high but shallow.** EY's 2019 Global FinTech Adoption Index (via Statista) found "Colombia is the Latin American country with the highest fintech adoption rate, with 76 percent of all digitally active population using two or more fintech services" (Peru second at 75%). The IDB–Finnovista series counts Colombia among the top-three LATAM fintech hubs; AI adoption among Colombian fintechs rose from 46.4% to 61.2% (Statista, 2023–24). But the *Columbia Emerging Markets Review*, citing the World Bank's 2023 "Digital Economy for LAC," warns "digital access can outpace users' comprehension," with "superficial" engagement.
- **Saving is weak and informal; present-orientation is high.** The most authoritative Colombia-specific behavioral source is **Reddy, Bruhn & Tan (2013), *Financial Capability in Colombia: Results from a National Survey on Financial Behaviors, Attitudes, and Knowledge*, World Bank** (nationally representative, n=1,526, with Banco de la República). Key data points: 94% reported budgeting but only 23% knew how much they spent in the prior week; 72% had no savings product and 45% had no financial products at all; only ~25% held a formal savings account while ~4–5% used informal rotating savings; 81% could not calculate a simple interest rate; and **55% indicated an orientation toward the present rather than the future**, correlated with self-identified impulsiveness. About 65% reported having been short of money for basic expenses, and 56% of those relied on borrowing from family or friends. CAF's Diana Mejía (via BBVA) summarizes the regional pattern: Latin Americans surveyed "do not save; and if they do so, they prefer to keep their money at home or save through informal means."
- **Financial education *can* work in Colombia, with the right delivery.** Attanasio, Bird, Cardona-Sosa & Lavado (2019), "Freeing Financial Education via Tablets: Experimental Evidence from Colombia" (NBER Working Paper 25929) — an RCT of the tablet-based LISTA program among Más Familias en Acción cash-transfer recipients — found "significant positive impacts on financial knowledge, attitudes, practices, and performance," largest for "poorer, less educated, and more rural populations," though longer-term *formal financial inclusion* gains were limited. Kast, Meier & Pomeranz (2018, *Journal of Development Economics* vol. 133:275–294) show in Chile that peer self-help groups raised savings deposits 3.5-fold (and that feedback text messages alone achieved similar effects), with time-inconsistent individuals benefiting most — relevant to designing accountability features for present-biased users.
- **Conspicuous/social-spending pressure is culturally salient.** Work on conspicuous consumption in emerging markets, including a Colombia case study (*Frontiers in Psychology*, 2021, Starbucks in Colombia), documents "strong pressure to excel socially" via consumption — meaning the "social" category in Colombia carries both genuine relational value *and* status-signaling overspending risk that the app should help users distinguish.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Keep the agency framing, but reposition it as a reflection tool, not a tracking taxonomy.**
- Retain the controllable-vs-uncontrollable distinction; it is the best-supported design choice (locus of control / financial self-efficacy literature). Frame the app's value proposition around *building perceived control*, which is the proven causal lever.
- Map the six agency categories onto users' natural "necessity" dimension (Fei, Bartels & Zhang 2024) rather than asking users to abandon intuitive type categories — e.g., let AI assign a type tag *and* an agency tag.
- **Benchmark that would change this:** if in-app A/B tests show the agency labels do not increase savings-rate or reduce discretionary overspending versus a simple type-based control within ~3 months, treat categorization as engagement scaffolding only and shift investment toward commitment features (Stage 4).

**Stage 2 — Add guardrails to "investment" and reflective friction to AI categorization.**
- Define "investment" narrowly with concrete inclusion/exclusion examples; add a lightweight confirmation ("Why is this an investment?") for high-value items to disrupt self-licensing (de Witt Huberts; Cheema & Soman).
- Keep AI auto-categorization for drudgery removal, but require a one-tap confirm/override so the agency-affirming reflective moment survives (de Ridder, 2021). Disclose AI use transparently — algorithm aversion is low in finance when accuracy and cost are clear.

**Stage 3 — Treat "social" (and ideally a time-saving tag) as recognized high-return spending, not guilt categories.**
- Use copy that affirms relational and time-saving spending's wellbeing benefits (Aknin et al.; Whillans et al.) while flagging status-signaling overspend risk specific to Colombian conspicuous-consumption norms.

**Stage 4 — Localize for Colombia and avoid the "budgeting app trap."**
- Design the income/committed categories for irregular, informal, biweekly-cash income (high informality, 55% present-orientation, 72% without savings products).
- Do **not** surface "money left to spend" as a headline number (budgeting-app-trap risk). Prefer rollover framing, less-precise balances, and just-in-time prompts. Consider peer-accountability or text-reminder features (Kast et al. 2018) for present-biased users.
- Pair categorization with light, well-timed education (Attanasio et al. 2019 shows it works for lower-income, rural users).

**Stage 5 — Measure objective outcomes, not engagement.**
- Track savings rate, debt levels, and end-of-period spending — not just DAU/category-tagging frequency. The Clarity Money and Peru RCTs both show engagement can rise with zero (or negative) financial benefit. **Benchmark:** a positive, statistically detectable effect on objective savings/spending is the only signal that should justify scaling the categorization feature.

## Caveats
- **Geographic skew:** Most categorization, prosocial-spending, and locus-of-control studies are US/European/Australian; direct Colombian behavioral RCTs on *agency-based categorization specifically* do not appear to exist. The Colombia evidence here is about saving behavior, present bias, and financial-education delivery, not about this exact categorization scheme.
- **Replication nuance on prosocial spending:** The effect is robust in large samples (Aknin et al. 2020, 2022) but has shown null results in at least one close replication (Kim et al. 2022) and delayed negative effects (Falk & Graeber 2020). Treat "social spending is good for wellbeing" as well-supported but conditional.
- **Working-paper status:** Fei, Bartels & Zhang (2024) and Attanasio et al. (2019) are working papers (BFI and NBER respectively); confirm final published versions before formal citation. Mantilla (2014, IAST WP 14-12) on Colombian interest-rate bias is also a working paper.
- **Citation-year note:** Cite the earmarking paper as Sussman & O'Brien **2016**, *Journal of Marketing Research* 53(5):790–803 (some secondary sources say 2015 due to early online release).
- **No "quincena"-specific academic study was located.** The claim that biweekly pay cycles shape Colombian spending is inferred from present-bias/short-horizon evidence (Reddy, Bruhn & Tan 2013), not from a dedicated study; validate with primary user research before designing around it.
- **Effect sizes are generally modest**, and the strongest single experimental result in this brief (the ~9,000-user Clarity Money RCT) is a *null* for budgeting's financial impact — the most important reason to position categorization as a means to perceived control and reflection rather than as an end in itself.
